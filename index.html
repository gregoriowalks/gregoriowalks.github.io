About the Book

The Man Who Walked Alone isn’t a manifesto. It isn’t a redemption arc or a hero’s journey wrapped in platitudes. It’s for readers who’ve stared into the abyss and found it uninterested in staring back.

Gregorio M. Roquentin dismantles illusions as a way of life—philosophical, political, and personal. When every system collapses under its own contradictions, what remains? Not faith. Not comfort. Only the discipline of continuing to walk.

This is a journey through entropy, memory, and intellectual sobriety: corporations simulating control, languages hollowed out, ideologies mistaken for identity. Gregorio observes, records, and advances. Without proclamations.

If Nietzsche played chess with Camus in an office designed by Kafka, the board might look like this book. There is no “happy ending”; only questions sharpened until they become tools.

About the Author

G. M. Absurdovich writes as an act of filtration—distilling collapse, contradiction, and solitude into precise clarity.

Influenced by Nietzsche, Camus, and Schopenhauer, he writes from lived systems breaking—currencies, bureaucracies, illusions.

Gregorio is not a protagonist but a test: a man who refuses to flinch, preach, or perform.

Themes

  • Absurdism without theatrics.
  • Solitude as rebellion.
  • The fragility of systems—currencies, corporations, ideologies.
  • The most dangerous illusions are mental.
  • Resilience comes not from certainty, but from questioning.
